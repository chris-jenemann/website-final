Backcountry Ski

Siyeh

Glacier, MT 5/13

Chris Jenemann 5/19/24

Henry, Elliot, and I will never have a good tour...

We set off to Glacier's west side, looking to capitalize on a small weather window and bag a bike-to-ski line off the Garden Wall, though we were quickly brought to a halt by several factors. Firstly, the 36-hour weather window we were relying on was rapidly shrinking, with rain and thunderstorms on the forecast for the next day. Secondly, the Park Service had closed Going-to-the-Sun Road on the west side, and did not update its website until the evening we arrived in Apgar. It seems that limiting funding to important government agencies might not be as good as they say. After realizing there was very little we could do, we enjoyed the beauty of our campground along the shore of Lake McDonald and pivoted to make the early drive to the East side in search of better weather.

We woke early the next morning to the flash and boom of lightning through the McDonald Valley. We ate quickly and got out of there, hoping the National Weather Service was right in their prediction of sun and warm temps on the other side of the continental divide. Before leaving, we did catch a sweet sunrise over the park.

After reaching St. Mary's and the eastern entrance to the park, we were pleasantly surprised to find that NWS was right with blue skies stretching out over the prairie. Though it was unclear how long the high pressure would last, with storm clouds looming in the distant west. We loaded our bikes with our skis and scooted up the upper section of the road to the avalanche closure, where we would head into the backcountry.

We had no real ski objective in mind; rather, we were just looking to get a peek at our intended line across the park via Siyeh Pass. We would bushwack up from the road, then roughly follow the Siyeh Pass trail to our destination.

After a quick bike and hike, we were on the snow, and there was a lot of it. It's amazing how the snowpack can seemingly triple in only a few hundred vertical feet. We made our way through the woods and onto the trail, where we found the footprints of someone who had decided to posthole up the trail. We would follow their prints for a good few miles. Along the trail, we would cross numerous creeks, each giving us a portal into the mammoth snowpack, at times, the snow being almost as tall as us.

The weather was holding, giving us amazing views of the surrounding mountains and all the possible ski lines they provided. The number of couloirs and snowfields was endless, giving us ample variety to choose from, though we decided to wait until our way out. We pushed on to the pass. At this point, it was late morning. It was warm, forcing us into our base layers and sunshirts. The snow was holding up well with no apparent signs of rapid warming or sun effect.

We reached the steep snowfields below the pass, and it became quicker to walk rather than ski, so we abandoned our skis and pushed up the pass. At this point, the storms hitting the west side were flowing over the divide, so we got up the pass and then quickly got out of there.

The pass failed to provide the view of the Salamander and Grinnel Glaciers that we were looking for, but we were not disappointed. Standing on the Continental Divide, we were treated to endless views of the park along with the brutal winds that came with the storm over the divide.

It began to rain and snow on us, so we forwent any detours on our way out, not even ripping skins. I’m sure some people would have found this activity quite ridiculous, as it amounted to a long walk through the snow without the conventional reward of downhill gliding. I did find some disappointment in this trip after being thwarted by the powers that be, both bureaucratic and atmospheric, but the disappointment was far outweighed by the views and fun memories with my buddies.
