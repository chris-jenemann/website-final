Canyoneering

Moonflower Canyon

Moab, UT 3/26

Chris Jenemann 5/29/26

Stuck Ropes and 70 Meter Free Hangs

Alright, this one was a bit of a disaster, but still pretty sweet, I mean, how can rappelling over 200 feet free hanging not be cool? After camping out in the middle of the Moab desert next to Jug Rock, the group and I relocated our camp to a spot on SR-128 right on the Colorado River. A pretty sweet spot we got really lucky to find, considering it was first-come, first-served. After dealing with group stuff, Fox Rowland and I set off to try our hand at canyoneering.

We parked our car at the mouth of Moonlight Canyon right outside of Moab and made the quick walk up the road towards our access point to the bluff. We got our first glimpse of some of the pictographs that are scattered across the wall of the canyon.

Along the way, we stopped to search for more pictographs on our way to the canyon scramble. It's pretty amazing to see this early artwork, only a few hundred meters away from a road.

At this point in the trip, we were pretty excited. We ran into some acquaintances from university on our way up, and the slick rock landscape made the going easy. I see why this part of the country is such a climbing paradise; everywhere you look, there are perfect lines up the striking red rock. We reached the final collection of pictographs which feature the "Moab Mammoth", though there is a debate about whether it's actually a bear with a fish or a mammoth. Regardless, the art was stunning, with my favorite being the deer/antelope glyph.

After leaving the pictographs, we made our way up a steep canyon and through a low 5th-class crux up to the bluff. We backtracked towards Moonflower Canyon, taking in the beautiful La Sals framed by desert towers. It's amazing how otherworldly the desert can feel when away from the crowds of people.

We reached the top of Moonflower Canyon, flaked the ropes, and prepared for the rappel down. This route consisted of two 70-meter mostly free-hanging rappels. Fox and I are relatively seasoned rock climbers, both having rappelled off routes before, but nothing this exposed.

I took the lead down the first one, and I definitely felt a bit shaky stepping off the cliff into space. Once I was hanging, the ride down was pretty sweet. Over 200 feet off the deck, I got some pretty amazing views of the lower canyon and the emerald Colorado River winding through Potash Canyon. Fox followed shortly after. Once both were back on the ground, we went to pull the ropes only to realize they had gotten stuck. Using two dynamic lead climbing ropes was definitely not the optimal choice for this, as the stretch made pulling them through the anchors hard and snag-prone. Regardless, we chose this route partially since all the rappels were able to be easily accessible through some easy scrambling.

After getting back to the anchors, we rerigged the ropes to prevent future snags, repeated the rappel, and pulled the rope.

We moved on to the second rappel, rigged the rope, and dropped down another 70 meters. The rope was stuck again, so we said screw it and just scrambled up again, dropped the rope, and scrambled out of the canyon. Honestly, I'm not sure how we managed to get stuck both times, but after doing some deep research, it seems that most serious canyoneers employ hyper-static lines with plate devices to do these long rappels. The static line makes pulling easier, and since they are often slippery, they get hung up less. Oh well, we made do and still had a blast.

We made our way out of the canyon and back to the car where we headed back to our campsite and enjoyed the evening on the river.
